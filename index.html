Research

Job Market Paper

Bargaining, Matching Efficiency and Unemployment Fluctuations

Paper (PDF)

Using business-cycle accounting, we show that a canonical search-and-matching model accounts for U.S. unemployment and vacancy fluctuations only if matching efficiency and the firm’s surplus share are procyclical and move together. To rationalize this comovement, we develop a model in which the worker is more likely to accept a more generous wage offer, so the firm trades off a larger share of the surplus against a higher probability of agreement. Higher productivity or lower uncertainty faced by the firm relaxes this trade-off, so the agreement probability, which plays the role of endogenous matching efficiency, rises together with the firm’s surplus share in booms. Quantitatively, the model can reproduce the firm’s measured surplus share while generating 85% of the volatility of measured matching efficiency. This microfounded comovement allows the model to account for the full volatility of unemployment and to match the joint fluctuations of unemployment and vacancies better than models with constant matching efficiency.

Working Papers

Reputational Bargaining with a Deterministic Deadline

Deadlines cause inefficient breakdowns when players hold out to pressure the other side into conceding in pursuit of a higher payoff. I extend the reputational bargaining model with a deterministic deadline, which provides a transparent setting for isolating this mechanism. The model nests Abreu and Gul’s (2000) infinite-horizon equilibrium as a special case when the deadline is sufficiently distant. However, equilibrium uniqueness and continuous trade patterns in the infinite-horizon and stochastic-deadline benchmarks do not extend to a deterministic deadline. When the deadline is relatively close, the model predicts a discontinuous, sharply U-shaped trade pattern, with agreement concentrated at the outset and at the deadline. The resulting inefficient breakdowns are entirely strategic and rational players may deliberately miss the deadline despite feasible trade. Multiple equilibria can arise when players are sufficiently patient and likely to be rational.

Work in Progress

A Filtering Approach to News-Based Measures: Application to the EPU Index

with Christian Gouriéroux, Dogukan Guney, Nour Meddahi, Serge Nyawa and Danfeng Yue

Text-based economic indices are widely used in empirical research, but their reliability depends on how they are built. Many of these indices are based on a dictionary of words and a set of newspapers, both chosen by the researcher. The resulting measures are sensitive to these choices and thus subject to measurement error. Drawing on the literature on volatility and realized volatility, we propose filtering methods, notably the Kalman filter, to reduce this error. We apply the approach to the Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) index of Baker, Bloom, and Davis (2016). In various regression analyses, the filtered index delivers more precise estimates and an improved empirical fit relative to the original measure. These findings show that filtering can make text-based economic indicators more reliable.

Heavy Tails and Professional Forecasters Analysis: Application to Information Rigidities and the Expectations Formation Process

with Nour Meddahi and Jihyun Kim

This paper examines linear regressions involving heavy-tailed variables with infinite variance. Although the statistical theory is well established, this issue is largely overlooked in the empirical literature. This is particularly true of studies relying on professional forecasts. To illustrate the importance of this issue, we revisit the influential work of Coibion and Gorodnichenko (2015), who develop a simple econometric framework for testing information rigidities in expectation formation. They show that forecast errors are predictable from revisions in expectations, a finding that contradicts the full-information rational expectations hypothesis. Their empirical results underscore the importance of information frictions and how these frictions vary over the business cycle. We estimate the tail behavior of the variables using several methods and find that many of them exhibit heavy tails. In the presence of heavy tails, the OLS estimator may fail to converge to the parameter of interest, converging instead to a non-degenerate random variable. Even when the OLS estimator is consistent, its asymptotic distribution is generally non-standard, often involving ratios of random variables with stable distributions. Moreover, standard bootstrap procedures are invalid in this setting, making subsampling a more appropriate alternative for statistical inference. These findings have important implications for the empirical results of Coibion and Gorodnichenko (2015), calling into question their robustness and, potentially, their validity. We employ estimation methods that remain consistent in the presence of heavy-tailed variables. These methods admit an instrumental variables interpretation, with instruments constructed as bounded functions of the heavy-tailed variables, such as their signs. Consistency requires only the existence of first moments, an assumption that we maintain and that is consistent with our empirical findings. We construct confidence intervals using subsampling procedures. Through Monte Carlo simulations, we demonstrate that both the proposed estimators and the associated confidence intervals exhibit good finite-sample properties. Using our alternative estimator, we find that the main parameter of interest in Coibion and Gorodnichenko (2015) is no longer statistically significant when symmetric confidence intervals are used but remains significant when equal-tailed and asymmetric confidence intervals are considered. We argue that asymmetric confidence intervals are more appropriate in this setting, as the limiting distribution of the estimator may itself be asymmetric.
